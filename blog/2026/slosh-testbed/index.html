Making a quadrotor slosh, and teaching a controller to calm it down

Created in November 22, 2026

2026   ·   control   aerospace   simulation   slosh   adaptive-control   ·   robotics   control

A note before we start. This post gives an overview of the work I took part in during my internship at NASA Langley Research Center. I wrote it on my own time; it is not sponsored or endorsed by NASA. The simulation in it is my own reconstruction from the published paper. It reproduces the paper’s results as closely as I could, but it is not a reflection of the original work.

Rockets are mostly fuel, and fuel moves. This post is about a small testbed that makes a drone behave like a rocket with sloshing propellant, and about the adaptive control idea that was used to calm it down. You can fly a simulation of it right here, push it into instability, and watch the controller step in.

1. Why slosh matters

A launch vehicle carries tens or hundreds of tonnes of liquid propellant. When the vehicle accelerates or steers, the liquid sloshes. The sloshing liquid pushes back on the tank, and the vehicle’s flight control system sees that push as an unmodeled disturbance. These unmodeled modes can have adverse effects on the flight controller, which can feed the sloshing dynamics and generate low-frequency oscillations.

This is not a hypothetical. On the second flight of SpaceX’s Falcon 1, Demo Flight 2 in March 2007, the second stage developed growing oscillations that were attributed to propellant slosh, and the vehicle lost control before it reached its target orbit. Slosh is also a standing concern for NASA’s Space Launch System (SLS), whose flight control system has to keep the slosh modes of its large tanks stable across a very wide range of flight conditions.

For typical launch vehicles the slosh modes sit at low frequencies, roughly 0.3 to 0.5 Hz. The rigid-body control loop has its gain crossover around 0.2 Hz. That is uncomfortably close: the slosh mode lives just above the frequency where the controller’s authority fades, so a modest increase in loop gain or a shift in phase can push it from damped to unstable.

SLS therefore includes an Adaptive Augmenting Control (AAC) system. It watches the control signal for the tell-tale signature of an oscillation the main controller is not handling, and lowers the loop gain when it sees one. The paper this post is based on tested that idea on a quadrotor [1].

2. The experiment

Slosh is hard to test on a real rocket, so the team I was part of during my internship at NASA Langley built a stand-in. The quadrotor (nicknamed “mama bird”) carries a hanging point mass: 0.05 kg on a rigid 1 m rod below the vehicle. The hanging mass swings like a pendulum, and the quadrotor’s attitude controller has to live with that swinging load. The pendulum’s natural frequency is

\[\omega_s \approx \sqrt{\frac{m_T\, g}{(m_T - m_p)\, L}} \approx 3.18\ \mathrm{rad/s} \approx 0.507\ \mathrm{Hz},\]

where \(m_T\) is the total mass. That is right in the 0.3 to 0.5 Hz band of a launch vehicle’s slosh modes, and it is the “slosh mode” of this testbed. The simulation uses the paper’s vehicle parameters: a 1.5207 kg quadrotor with a 0.241 m arm, inertias of 0.0211, 0.0216 and 0.01455 kg m², and rotor thrust and torque coefficients of 5.8597e-6 and 2.925e-9.

On the real testbed, a Vicon motion-capture system1 measured position and attitude, and the vehicle’s IMU supplied angular rates. The pendulum angle was not measured or fed back: just as on a rocket, the slosh is unmodelled and unobservable to the controller, and it has to be handled through the vehicle’s own motion.

The testbed has one more trick: a gain shift knob. It multiplies the outer-loop gain on purpose. Raising it moves the loop’s high-frequency gain margin toward zero, until the slosh mode at about 0.5 Hz becomes unstable. That is how the team created a controlled version of a slosh instability on demand, safely, and then tested whether the AAC could suppress it.

In the simulation the quadrotor is a full 6-degree-of-freedom rigid body with a quaternion attitude. The pendulum is integrated as a unit vector on a sphere, so nothing special happens at the straight-down position. Motors have a first-order lag. The model is integrated with a fixed-step RK4 at 1 kHz, and the controller runs at 100 Hz.

3. The controller

The controller is two nested SISO PD loops per horizontal axis (pitch and roll):

  • an inner loop that holds the quadrotor’s attitude, and
  • an outer loop that turns position and velocity errors into an attitude command.

Altitude and yaw are held by simple separate loops. The gain shift multiplies the outer-loop output. Crucially, nothing in the controller measures the pendulum.

The Nichols chart below is the outer loop of this simulation’s designed gains. The paper reports the margins of its real controller (its Table 1); the table compares them with the ones I designed.

Quantity R-1 Table 1 This simulation (pitch and roll)
Phase margin 22° near 0.2 Hz 24.4° at 0.205 Hz
High-frequency gain margin 2.6 (8.3 dB) near 0.525 Hz 1.34 (2.6 dB) at 0.513 Hz
Low-frequency gain margin none none

The phase margin and the shape of the loop land close to the paper. The high-frequency gain margin does not, on purpose. In the simulation the slosh mode goes unstable when the gain shift passes about 1.34, comfortably below 2.0. I chose that deliberately: it makes the unstable scenarios clearly visible in a short interactive demo. It is a teaching choice and not a claim about the real vehicle, whose margins are in the paper.

4. Adaptive Augmenting Control

The AAC adds one extra number to each of the pitch and roll loops: a forward-loop gain \(K_T\) that normally sits at 1. When \(K_T\) is applied, it multiplies the outer-loop output. The AAC changes it according to

\[\dot k_T = -a_0\,(k_T - 1) \;+\; b_e\, e_{\mathrm{ref}}^2\,(k_{\max} - k_T) \;-\; b_s\, y_{SD}\,(k_T - k_{\min}),\]

with \(k_T\) kept between \(k_{\min}\) and \(k_{\max}\). Each term has a job:

  • Leakage, \(-a_0 (k_T - 1)\), pulls the gain back toward 1 so the AAC does not stay in a reduced-gain state once the oscillations have subsided.
  • Reference error (the up-band term, \(b_e\)) would raise the gain when the vehicle does not follow its commands. In the testbed experiments it is off, so \(b_e = 0\) and the AAC is down-band only.
  • Spectral damper, \(-b_s\, y_{SD}\,(k_T - k_{\min})\), lowers the gain when it detects oscillation near the slosh frequency.

The spectral damper signal \(y_{SD}\) is built from the control signal. It is a high-pass filter that keeps the content around 0.5 Hz, followed by squaring (so it measures oscillation power) and a low-pass filter. A flat-lining, quiet control signal gives \(y_{SD} \approx 0\). A growing oscillation near the slosh frequency makes \(y_{SD}\) rise. When it crosses a threshold, the simulation flags an anomaly; that is the detector. Lowering \(K_T\) lowers the loop gain, which brings the slosh mode back inside its stability margin.

The AAC has three modes in the simulation. Off: \(K_T\) is fixed at 1 and the detector is hidden. Monitor: the AAC and the detector compute and show everything, but \(K_T\) is not applied, so the flight is identical to Off. Engaged: \(K_T\) is applied. This mirrors how the flight test was run: the AAC watched first and was engaged later.

5. Try it yourself

The widget below is a single continuous flight. It starts at nominal gains with the pendulum already swinging (0.2 rad), and you steer it through three regimes without ever resetting the vehicle.

A guided walkthrough

The three regime buttons over the 3D view are labelled with the figure of the paper they reproduce, qualitatively:

  1. Nominal · Fig 12. The gain shift is 1 and the AAC is off. The controller damps the slosh, slowly: the swing amplitude decays but does not vanish quickly. Nothing is flagged.
  2. Gain shift · Fig 13. The outer-loop gains are raised on purpose, to 2.3 times their nominal value, to drive the slosh mode unstable. The AAC switches to Monitor: it computes \(K_T\) and the detector, but does not apply them. Watch the swing amplitude grow, with the mass’s dominant motion migrating from one horizontal axis to the other. The detector trips a few seconds after the shift, in my reconstruction about 3 s later, while the swing is still moderate.
  3. AAC engaged · Fig 14. The gain shift stays where it is, and the AAC starts applying \(K_T\). Like the flight test, the computed \(K_T\) is applied at once, as a step, rather than being faded in. The growth stops and the amplitude begins to fall. In the charts you see \(K_T\) drop on both axes (more on pitch than on roll), and then slowly recover toward 1 as the slosh calms down.

The regime buttons act on the running flight, exactly like the replay. Tapping one starts the flight if it is paused, ramps the gains over about 1.5 s, and the motion and the charts carry on; the vehicle is never reset. So you can stay in Gain shift as long as you like, let the oscillation grow as large as you want, and then engage the AAC. If the pendulum is nearly still when you tap Gain shift, the widget also gives it a small disturbance so you do not have to wait for the instability to show up. That disturbance gets its own label on the charts.

Three things help you follow what happens:

  • The info banner along the bottom edge of the 3D view says what the current phase is, what you should see happen, and which paper figure it corresponds to. For Gain shift it says plainly that the gains are being raised on purpose to make the fuel slosh. When the detector trips, the trip message appears there too.
  • The charts inside the widget show the pendulum angle, \(K_T\) and the spectral damper output \(y_{SD}\). Full screen shows all six charts, larger, including the mass position, the quad’s roll and pitch and the motor commands.
  • Markers on every chart show each regime transition, labelled in full screen (“Gain shift → 2.3”, “AAC monitoring”, “AAC engaged”), and the background shading shows which regime you are in. The colours are chosen to be distinguishable under common forms of colour-vision deficiency.
  • The detector-trip marker is drawn differently from the transition markers, so you can tell at a glance “the reader changed something” from “the detector noticed something”.

Two more buttons: Replay the paper resets the vehicle and then runs the sequence Nominal → Gain shift → AAC engaged automatically, with timing tuned so that it matches the figure above. Tapping any regime button during a replay cancels the remaining automatic steps and hands control back to you. Restart is the only control that resets the vehicle: it returns to the starting state and schedules nothing.

The widget plays like a video: tap the 3D view to pause or resume (a drag still orbits the camera), and use the small bar for speed and full screen. It follows this site’s light and dark theme, and it does not autoplay if your system asks for reduced motion. Inside the widget, the collapsed Fine-tune section holds the raw gain-shift slider, the AAC mode selector and the parameter panel, if you want to explore outside the three regimes.

6. Flight-test results

The paper’s flight test (its section V.B) follows the story above, but at the scale of a real experiment. The gain shift was ramped up to 2 over 20 s with the AAC in Monitor. The slosh grew into a large rotary motion of about 20°. The AAC was then engaged at t = 100 s, and the paper reports the slosh suppressed within about 10 s.

The figure below is the same sequence in my simulation. It is generated from this project’s model, not copied from the paper.

The simulation reproduces the shape of the story and not the speed of the ending. The slosh grows to about 0.37 rad (21°) before engagement, the detector trips at about 55 s while the AAC is still only watching, and engagement turns the growth around. But in my reconstruction the amplitude then comes down slowly (from about 0.37 rad at 100 s to about 0.28 rad at 130 s), not within 10 s as in the real flight. The cause is that the controller’s gains, which I had to design, change the slosh damping only modestly even when \(K_T\) is at its minimum. I report this as a known difference rather than tuning it away.

7. What’s reconstructed

This simulation is a reconstruction to tell a story. The paper gives the dynamics and the physical parameters (its Eq. 1 to 5 and Table 2), and I used those directly. It does not give the controller gains, the motor time constant, the controller rate, the AAC filters and tuning, or the detection threshold. I had to design, assume or tune them, and I cannot claim any of them match the real testbed. The full list, with where each value comes from, is in the project repository (reconstruction.md).
